# Harden the Engine, Change the Yardstick

Kaibot's engine is better built than most home-grown trading bots, but it is not yet production grade, and its trading rule does not beat simply holding the S&P 500 fund (SPY). The order core is sound: every order is written to a local journal before it is sent, order IDs are deterministic so a retry cannot double-buy, and fills are booked exactly once. The weak points sit around that core. Reading the code at commit `3f9bfee` found **two ways the engine could sell the whole paper book when it should not or fail to sell it when told to**, **five confirmed ways a weekly or monthly rebalance is silently skipped**, a single free Supabase project that the start-up, the lease, the watchdog and the dead-man switch all depend on, and a 3,066-line core file that still runs the retired intraday rule in front of the live one. On the trading side, **no tested rule beat SPY**: the live rule earned 8.24% a year against SPY's 14.37% over 2017 to 2026, with about half the drawdown. Part of that verdict is the rule and part is the yardstick, because the pass test compares a rule that sits mostly in cash with an index that is always fully invested. The evidence supports a realistic target of a return within about 1.5 points a year of SPY with a 20% to 40% shallower drawdown, reached by holding an index core and adding small, counted, pre-registered sleeves on top; it does not support reliably beating SPY's raw return without leverage. The plan below fixes safety first (about twenty small tickets), then the evaluation and portfolio layer, then automated fault-finding tests and bounded AI uses, and only then the paid server, multi-user and mobile work, which is gated by a German legal opinion, a company, and Alpaca's approval and is not an engineering problem alone.

**How to read the labels.** "Confirmed" means a researcher read it in the code; nothing in this report was reproduced by running the engine or a backtest. "Suspected" means it follows from the code but depends on something unverified, usually how Alpaca behaves. Code findings cite a file and line at commit `3f9bfee`. Sourced facts carry a link. "Proposal" marks the research team's own design suggestions, and "own calculation" marks figures the team computed itself; neither has been tested. Several result files exist only on the owner's Mac and were not read, which limits some conclusions and is itself a finding.

## The verdict: a sound order core inside fragile scheduling and an unpassable test

The scorecard below is the research team's assessment, area by area. Grades compare Kaibot with what the leading open-source engines and the industry control lists describe, not with other hobby bots.

| Area | Grade today | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sending orders and booking fills (main path) | Strong | Journal first, deterministic IDs, unknown orders never re-sent blind, fills booked once |
| Local journal and durability | Strong | Append-only SQLite, write-ahead logging with full sync, changes refused by triggers |
| Getting a decision made and sent on time | Weak | Late start, missed window, or one failing earlier step loses the rebalance with no retry |
| Forced exits (book stop, "Flatten and stop") | Weak | The biggest sale needs the least evidence; the kill switch is not retried on failure |
| Recovery and liveness | Medium to weak | Engine cannot start without Supabase; watchdog treats "cannot read" as "engine dead" |
| Ledger and reconciliation | Medium | Zero-tolerance share checks, but two of four checks are not wired in live; clearing needs a terminal |
| Data for decisions | Strong | Point-in-time, coverage and integrity gates, fails closed |
| Prices used for risk during the day | Weak | One thin source, no staleness limit, no second opinion |
| Code health | Medium to weak | Three order paths, retired code on the critical path, `core.py` grew 2.5 times in nine days |
| Security | Split | Dashboard side tight; engine side trusts one all-powerful key held by several helpers |
| Research process | Strong | Pre-registration, point-in-time universe, counted trials, one simulator |
| Research yardstick | Weak | The pass test measures how invested a rule is, not whether it has skill |
| The trading rule itself | Faithful, uncompetitive | A correct copy of a published rule that lagged SPY by 6.1 points a year |
| Multi-user and mobile | Not started | One run, one key, one lease, one journal; legal route unresolved |

Ten findings matter most. First, the book's "run end" (sell everything and retire the rules after a 13%, 21% or 27% fall from the peak) can be triggered by one unconfirmed price, because the peak is raised on a single reading and both confirming checks can read the same stale bar. Second, "Flatten and stop" marks itself done even when the sale was incomplete, and nothing retries. Third, an engine that first comes up after 09:30 New York time on a trading day never takes that session's decision, which for the monthly rule means a month on the old book. Fourth, the send to the broker is the tenth step of each minute's loop and any earlier step that fails aborts it, on each of the seven minutes of the 15:50 to 15:57 window. Fifth, a Supabase hiccup makes the watchdog cancel the engine's own working buys, which are then excluded from the carry-over. Sixth, about one minute of network trouble at any time of day blocks all buying until the next session. Seventh, the pass test (Gate B, mark 2) is failed "almost by construction" by any rule that is not near fully invested, so it cannot tell a small real edge from none. Eighth, with about ten rule families tested at a 10% level and no correction, one "pass" on the random-control test is expected by chance. Ninth, automatic trading in other people's accounts without per-trade approval is licensed portfolio management in Germany and the EU, which changes what the multi-user product can be. Tenth, both app stores require a legal entity, not an individual, for trading apps.

What changed since the 12 to 13 November research is worth stating, because it shows the direction of travel. The order-path defects found then (order row written after the broker call, a non-atomic loop, rejects aborting the scan, no partial fills, no reconciliation, manual daily start) are fixed on the new multi-day path, and a real order manager, a portfolio builder and a simulated broker now exist (`engine/orders.py`, `risk/portfolio.py`, `broker/sim.py`). That research forecast a momentum core landing between two points behind and one point ahead of SPY; the honest run came in 6.1 points behind, well outside that band, while the drawdown promise held. The pattern of the last nine days has been to fix each incident by adding a guarded special case ([#333](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/333), [#363](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/363), [#392](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/392)). Each fix is correct; the cost is the number of states the seven-minute send window can now be in, which has become the main source of risk.

## Where the engine is weak: seven-minute windows, one price source, one Supabase project

### Five confirmed ways to miss a rebalance, and none retries

The engine decides after the close and sends DAY market orders between 15:50 and 15:57 New York time on the next session (`engine/execution.py:71-79`, `engine/live_daily.py:933-936`). Sending twice is hard: the client order ID is built from run, strategy, symbol, date and purpose, an open order for the same decision is reused, and an order with no answer becomes "unknown" and blocks that symbol until a lookup resolves it (`engine/orders.py:91-100, 302-343`). The opposite failure, sending late or never, is easy.

A late start loses the decision: the after-close decision code runs only when the market is closed, so an engine that comes up after the open finds no decision and writes a note instead of making one (`engine/core.py:1390-1402`, `engine/live_daily.py:1218-1227, 1472-1489`) [confirmed]. A missed send window loses both the sales and the buys, because the carry-over logic needs the original send record to exist (`live_daily.py:960, 1322-1324`); the module's own header admits this (`live_daily.py:51-52`) [confirmed]. The send sits behind nine other steps, several of which can raise an error and are not wrapped, including a market-data call for the retired intraday universe with a 30-second timeout, five attempts and no overall deadline (`core.py:1389-1435`, `engine/alpaca.py:24`) [confirmed]. The watchdog counts an unreadable heartbeat as infinitely old and cancels open buys; a buy cancelled by the venue is then left out of the carry-over (`engine/watchdog.py:89-94, 183-197`, `engine/daily.py:384-386`) [confirmed]. Three failed loop passes in a row, about a minute of trouble, set a kill flag that is only cleared at the next session start (`core.py:92, 492-499, 1516-1518`) [confirmed]. Two calendar traps add to this: the macro-event calendar halts all buying after its `covers_until` date if nobody extends it (`core.py:1520-1523`), and the Mac's fixed 15:00 local start falls after the US open in the weeks when US and EU daylight-saving dates differ [suspected, the start-hour default was not read].

The auditors' reading is that carry-over repairs "sent but unfilled" and nothing else. "Never decided", "never sent" and "cancelled by a fail-safe" all need one idea: **the latest decision's targets stay due until they are met or replaced by a newer decision**. That is the single most valuable structural change, together with giving the multi-day path its own small loop (clock, sync, send) that does not run behind intraday steps.

### The largest forced sale needs the least evidence

Entering a position requires every check to pass. The book's run end, which sells every position at market and retires every rule until a manual re-enable, needs two readings of one price source (`core.py:738-770`). The price is the last IEX minute bar of the day, however old, or the broker's current price (`core.py:783-790`); IEX is one exchange's slice of trading, so a large-cap stock can go minutes without a print there. The peak is journalled as soon as one reading is half a cent higher and can never come down (`core.py:686-689`), and the two breach checks 60 seconds apart can read the same bar (`core.py:704-722`) [confirmed]. A bad print in a large holding, or a split the ledger has not booked yet (old share count, new price), fits the trigger. The same unguarded prices feed the daily stop (`core.py:1977-1989`).

"Flatten and stop" has a hole on the other side. When the flatten leaves anything unsold it raises an error; the generic handler then marks the command as applied with the error text, the run stays "running", and no later pass retries (`core.py:1193-1215, 2652-2657`) [confirmed]. Commands are also applied while the market is closed, so the sale waits as DAY market orders for the next open, which the book-end code deliberately avoids for itself (`core.py:676-678`). Two smaller order paths sit beside the order manager without its guarantees. Multi-day flatten sales use a timestamped ID and book only fully filled orders, so a partly filled sale that is then cancelled is never booked (`core.py:931-955, 1019-1039`; the only test of the cancelled case asserts no fill, `tests/test_md_exit_restart.py:83`) [confirmed]. If the submit call fails after Alpaca accepted the order, a second sale under a new ID is attempted and stopped only by the venue [suspected sequence]. No check of quote, halt status or the day's move is made before near-close market orders go out, and the share count was sized at the previous close (`live_daily.py:1271-1284`, `engine/targets.py:99-104`) [confirmed absent].

### Local-first for state, Supabase-first for staying alive

The journal is solid: SQLite in write-ahead mode with `synchronous=FULL`, one connection under a lock, and triggers that refuse updates and deletes (`engine/journal.py:79-97, 122-127`). An independent review of SQLite's settings confirms that this combination is durable, adds that write-ahead mode with the weaker `NORMAL` setting is not, and notes that on macOS the `fullfsync` option is off by default and should be turned on ([Ayer](https://www.agwa.name/blog/post/sqlite_durability)). The gaps are around the journal. The engine exits if it cannot take its lease from Supabase or verify the journal's identity there (`engine/lease.py:98-106`, `engine/__main__.py:292-310`), so a Supabase outage at start-up means no engine while the dead-man switch, which also lives in Supabase, is down too [confirmed]. The replicator that copies the journal to Supabase stops at the first row Supabase permanently refuses and retries it forever; the dashboard and the dead-man's copy of the book go stale while trading continues, and no alarm follows (`journal.py:663-688, 778-803`) [confirmed]. After "run ended, then restart" with a flatten sale not yet booked, the routine that would book it is unreachable and a new-run command waits forever (`core.py:1184-1187, 1403-1410`) [confirmed]. The engine never compares its clock with the broker's; a Mac clock more than three minutes slow would send DAY market orders after 16:00 [suspected, Alpaca's handling not verified]. An order is declared lost after two lookups a minute apart return nothing, and a new attempt may follow; if Alpaca's lookup lags, two orders exist [suspected].

### Reconciliation blocks buys, never sells, but half its checks are switched off live

Shares are compared with zero tolerance at start-up, at session start and every minute; cash within the larger of $1 and 0.05% of the pot. A difference blocks buys and always lets sales through, so the book cannot be locked in (`engine/reconcile.py:55-88, 211-220`) [confirmed]. Two things weaken this. The checks for fills on orders the engine does not know ("foreign fill" and "unbooked") run only when the order manager is passed in, and live it never is (`reconcile.py:127-136`, `core.py:434-450`) [confirmed]. Clearing a difference needs a command that only a terminal on the Mac or a privileged database insert can create, because the dashboard function accepts four command kinds and "accept reconcile" is not one (`migrations/0005_dashboard_controls.sql:5-9`, `core.py:1195-1197`) [confirmed]. For a non-technical owner that means buys stay blocked and a weekly or monthly book slowly empties. Corporate actions are read ten days back only (`core.py:1629-1636`), and a held stock with a persistently bad price bar freezes the whole rule's decision, sales included, until someone repairs the store (`live_daily.py:857-865`).

### Good decision data, unguarded risk marks, growing state

The decision input is well protected: a session's data is used only once SPY's bar for it is available, at least 97% of yesterday's stocks must have a close today, and structural errors or more than 1% bad bars stop the decision (`live_daily.py:286-381`). The daily store is refreshed by two scheduled jobs and the engine never fetches it itself, so two failed refreshes become a missed decision (`scripts/install_service.sh:111`, `live_daily.py:315`). No second data source cross-checks either the daily bars or the session marks. State only grows: there is no compaction of the journal, the in-memory store keeps 390 ledger rows a session, and several routines scan the full journal several times per minute in the send window (`journal.py:239-244`, `live_daily.py:548-571`, `core.py:641-647`); [#238](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/238) already recorded a symptom. No size or timing was measured, so the magnitude is unknown.

### Three order paths and one all-powerful key

`engine/core.py` grew from 1,244 to 3,066 lines in nine days. It holds three ways to place orders (the retired intraday path, the order manager, the flatten sales), three ID schemes and three start-up recovery routines, so every new guarantee must be written three times (`core.py:397-404, 948-1073, 2349-2373`). Event names are built dynamically, and five database migrations in one week were needed to keep the dashboard's whitelist in step (`core.py:1212`, `docs/ops/migrations_pending.md`). At least seventeen behaviour switches are read from environment variables across modules. By search of test names, no test covers a failing market-data read inside the loop, a late start on a fill day, a missed window, a watchdog cancel followed by carry-over, clock skew or a poisoned replicator row; test contents were not read, so some paths may be covered under other names.

On security the dashboard is tight: its command function checks the owner, accepts four kinds and requires an idempotency key (`migrations/0005_dashboard_controls.sql:2-13`). The engine side is not. One service-role key with full rights on every table is held by the engine, the watchdog, the price relay and at least one script (`migrations/0004_engine_service_role.sql:3-7`, `scripts/price_stream.py:642-644`). With it a process can insert any command, take the lease, write a fresh heartbeat so the dead-man never fires, or replace the owner row. A new-run command is not bounded in the engine: the $1,000 to $100,000 limit is a database constraint met only later by the replicator, which the bad row would then stall (`core.py:1187, 1217-1219`) [confirmed]. The first user to sign in on an empty owner table becomes the owner (`migrations/0002_dashboard_access.sql:53-60`). Trading is fixed to Alpaca's paper endpoint (`broker/alpaca.py:3, 122-123`), which bounds the damage today and is the reason these must be closed before any other account is connected.

## The algorithm and its test: an honest process measuring the wrong thing

### What trades, and what the ten-year tests say

One rule trades on paper, labelled as having failed its gate: `trend_momentum`, a faithful, untuned copy of Andreas Clenow's "Stocks on the Move". Each week it ranks S&P 500 members by the slope of a 90-day price trend line times how well the line fits, buys from the top 20% only while SPY is above its 200-day average, sizes each position inversely to its recent daily range, holds up to 30 names, uses no per-stock stop, and sells when a stock leaves the top 20% or breaks its 100-day average (`src/kaibot/strategies/momentum.py`, `docs/prereg/trend_momentum.md`). Every other rule is research only.

| Rule | Return a year | Sharpe | Worst drawdown | Average invested | Rank against random picks | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPY, dividends included | 14.37% | 0.83 | 33.78% | 100% | n/a | [#370](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/370) |
| `trend_momentum` (live on paper) | 8.24% | 0.59 | 18.27% | not in repo | not in repo | `docs/decisions/15_engine_v3.md` s.11 |
| `post_earnings_week` | 6.01% | 0.58 | 24.41% | 36.1% | 77th percentile | [#370](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/370) |
| `news_gap_week` | 2.76% | 0.54 | 13.94% | 11.0% | 88th percentile | [#372](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/372) |
| `news_gap_week` without news (control) | 0.07% | 0.04 | not in repo | not in repo | n/a | [#372](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/372) |
| `spy_noise_band` through the engine | −0.08% | −0.17 | 1.82% | about 13% (owner's figure) | passes | [#202](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/202) |

(Sharpe is return per unit of risk; the percentile compares the rule with at least 500 portfolios that copy its timing and sizing but pick names at random.) Three further rules have results only on the owner's Mac (`weekly_fall_rebound` 9.7% a year with a 47.6% drawdown, `pullback_uptrend` 4.82%, `ath_breakout` 5.36%), and the two intraday stock rules lost money per trade. Those figures were supplied in the assignment and not verified in the repository.

The auditors sort the results into three groups, as their own reading. No edge: the opening-range breakout, intraday news continuation, the filing flags, the pullback and all-time-high rules as built. Market exposure with a poor trade-off: `trend_momentum`, whose Sharpe of 0.59 against SPY's 0.83 means lower risk-adjusted return, not only lower return, and `weekly_fall_rebound`, which is near zero exposure most of the time and jumps to fully invested in crashes. Small, possibly real stock-picking edge: `news_gap_week` has the most coherent evidence, with a forty-fold gap to its no-news twin, but rests on 532 trades and on today's surviving companies; `post_earnings_week` cannot be told apart from random names held on the same days; and `spy_noise_band` shows a real gross edge of 3.7 basis points a trade (a basis point is one hundredth of a percent) over 1,109 trades that the cost model exactly cancels ([#202](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/202)). None is statistically significant once the number of rules tried is counted.

### Gate B fails part-time rules by arithmetic, and one cost bucket decided the SPY rule

Gate B has six marks that must all hold (`src/kaibot/evaluation/gate_v2.py`). Mark 2 requires the whole account to earn within two points of a fully invested SPY with at most three quarters of its drawdown, or one point more with similar drawdown. Marks 4 and 5 re-test mark 2 at doubled costs and on the point-in-time universe, so a single miss shows up as three failures. In every test idle cash earns 0% (`src/kaibot/evaluation/mtm.py`). The auditors' arithmetic: a rule that is 36% invested on average needs about 34% a year on its invested money to reach 12.37% on the account, and one that is 11% invested needs over 100%. The project's own design document says such a rule "fails mark 2 almost by construction" (`docs/research/event_driven/design.md` s.2.8). For `trend_momentum` the miss is on return alone (8.24% against a 12.37% hurdle); its drawdown passes with room to spare (18.27% against a limit of 25.3%), which suggests the rule is more protected than the gate requires.

The methodology errs in both directions, in the auditors' judgement. It is too strict where it charges SPY the generic large-stock cost bucket of 1.75 basis points a side, when SPY's quoted spread is normally one cent on a share price of several hundred dollars (`src/kaibot/broker/costs_v2.py`; the cent figure is the auditor's knowledge of the instrument, not a repository number). The SPY rule's own pre-registration said it needs fills under about half a basis point a side, and at that cost it earns 2.7 basis points a trade with a t-statistic of 2.31. So "the edge is smaller than realistic costs" is established for the generic bucket only, and a free check exists in the paper account's own fill-versus-quote record. Even then the risk gate holds a single instrument to about 15% of the account, which caps the rule at roughly 0.4% to 0.5% a year whatever its edge. It is too lenient where the random-control mark has no correction for the number of rules tried: with about ten families tested at a one-in-ten level, the chance that at least one passes with no skill is about 65%, and `weekly_fall_rebound`'s 94.6th percentile is exactly such an event. The deflated Sharpe ratio (a correction for the number of attempts, defined by [Bailey and López de Prado](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)) is computed but not judged, and counts 8,286 near-identical configurations of the retired dip rule against unrelated new rules, which makes it unpassable as built. No stretch of history is held back for a final check. What is sound should be kept: pre-registration before code, point-in-time index membership, raw prices for orders, one simulator, doubled-cost stress, and negative results kept.

### Why long-only stock picking lost to the index in this decade

The outside evidence says the outcome was the expected one. Across all US stocks since 1926 about 4% of firms account for all the wealth created above Treasury bills ([Bessembinder, via ASU](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)), so any portfolio that holds a subset and underweights the few giants tends to trail. This decade was a record case: the ten largest stocks rose from about 19% of the S&P 500 at the end of 2015 to 40.7% at the end of 2025, and over the three years to the end of 2025 the cap-weighted index beat its equal-weighted version by about 32 points ([RBC Wealth Management](https://www.rbcwealthmanagement.com/en-us/insights/the-great-narrowing-sp-500-concentration)). Professionals fared no better: 79% of active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025 ([InvestmentNews](https://investmentnews.com/equities/active-managers-stumble-again-in-2025-as-large-caps-dominate/265541)), and a majority beat it in only 3 of 25 years ([S&P Indexology](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2026/05/25/measuring-the-managers/)). Published anomalies lose about half their return within ten years of the sample's end, and restricting them to tradable large stocks removes a further 25% to 35% ([Chen and Zimmermann](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.13623)).

The research team's own calculation from Alpaca's adjusted monthly bars (December 2016 to 20 November 2026, no costs, not a published source) makes the same point with products anyone can buy. SPY compounded at 13.7%; the equal-weight fund RSP at 11.1%; the momentum fund MTUM at 13.6%; quality (QUAL) 13.3%; minimum volatility (USMV) 10.0%. Only SPMO, which multiplies momentum by company size and so kept the giants, was clearly ahead at 17.3%. A sharp sell-off in November 2026 moved MTUM from 0.8 points ahead of SPY to level in three weeks, which shows how much any ten-year verdict depends on the end date. The auditors list nine structural reasons Kaibot cannot beat SPY as designed; the largest are cash drag (every point of idle cash cost about 0.14 points a year, so the Balanced level's 60% exposure cap gives up about 5.7 points before any signal is seen), weights that underweight the mega caps, a market filter that turns drawdown protection into missed recoveries, and judging each rule alone in a cash account.

### What a realistic target is

The research team's synthesis, to be read as ranges and not forecasts: for a long-only account without borrowing and on free data, a defensible goal is a return within about ±1.5 points a year of SPY over a decade, a Sharpe ratio 0.05 to 0.15 higher, and a drawdown 20% to 40% shallower if 15% to 30% of the book is in assets that do not move with stocks and portfolio volatility is capped. A stock-picking sleeve can plausibly add 0.5 to 1.5 points a year at a tracking error (how far the account wanders from SPY) of 2% to 4%, but showing that at the usual statistical bar takes 25 years or more, so ten years of data cannot confirm or reject it. Selection among rules therefore has to rest on decades of published evidence and low turnover, not on which rule had the best decade. Beating SPY's raw return reliably without leverage or short selling is not supported by any evidence found. The owner's confirmed goal, "market-like return with smaller drawdowns" (`docs/decisions/15_engine_v3.md` s.1), is reachable; "more than SPY" is not a responsible promise.

## How real platforms and working strategies do it

### The shape matches the best engines; the depth does not yet

The two open-source engines that are production grade for live trading share one pattern: a single core used for backtest and live with clock, data and broker swapped per environment, a single-threaded decision loop, a separate risk stage every order passes, and recovery from the broker at start-up ([NautilusTrader architecture](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/architecture/); [LEAN](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/lean-engine/getting-started.md)). Kaibot has that shape. Neither engine is a drop-in: NautilusTrader's integration list has no Alpaca adapter and its core is mid-rewrite ([README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/nautechsystems/nautilus_trader@develop/README.md)), and QuantConnect's free tier includes no live nodes ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/cloud-platform/live-trading/deployment.md)). The earlier decision to keep a custom engine and copy patterns stands.

In two respects Kaibot is ahead. It journals an order's intent before sending, whereas NautilusTrader's event store does not gate dispatch on a durable commit ([NautilusTrader live docs](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/live/)). And it keeps its own ledger, whereas LEAN on restart adopts the broker's holdings and loses all strategy state unless the user saves it ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/cloud-platform/live-trading/deployment.md)). The table lists where it is behind; the right-hand column is the auditors' comparison.

| What the reference engines and control lists have | Source | Kaibot today |
|---|---|---|
| A global trading state: active, reducing only, or halted | [NautilusTrader execution](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/execution/) | Several separate kill flags and entry blocks; no single switch |
| A mandatory pre-trade stage: size and value caps, price sanity, rate limit that never blocks cancels | [NautilusTrader execution](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/execution/); [FIA 2024](https://fia.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/FIA_WP_AUTOMATED%20TRADING%20RISK%20CONTROLS_FINAL_0.pdf) | Caps at sizing time; no price or halt check before near-close market orders |
| Orders blocked when market data is stale or anomalous | [FIA 2024](https://fia.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/FIA_WP_AUTOMATED%20TRADING%20RISK%20CONTROLS_FINAL_0.pdf) | Decision data guarded; session marks not |
| A missing or failed position report is never read as "flat"; a targeted single-order query before any "not found" conclusion; recently touched orders exempt | [NautilusTrader reconciliation](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/execution/reconciliation) | First rule holds; "lost" is declared after two lookups without checking open orders or fills |
| Continuous checks of in-flight orders, open orders and positions | [NautilusTrader reconciliation](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/execution/reconciliation) | Runs every minute, but without the order manager's fill checks |
| One order path; duplicate fills detected by trade ID and overfills rejected | [NautilusTrader execution](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/execution/) | True on one of three paths |
| A kill switch reachable independently of the engine that cancels working orders | [FIA 2024](https://fia.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/FIA_WP_AUTOMATED%20TRADING%20RISK%20CONTROLS_FINAL_0.pdf); [RTS 6, Art. 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/589/data.html) | Watchdog and dead-man exist; both depend on the same Supabase project |
| A live equity curve compared with a parallel backtest as a drift alarm | [LEAN reconciliation](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/writing-algorithms/live-trading/reconciliation.md) | Gate C tolerance defined; live-only branches are not in the research loop |
| Alerts within five seconds of the event | [RTS 6, Art. 16](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/589/data.html) | Healthchecks pings; replication failure raises no alarm |

Those control lists come from rules that bind brokers and investment firms, not a private paper trader; they are a template. The Knight Capital case shows why each line exists: in 45 minutes on 1 August 2012 the firm's router sent more than 4 million orders and lost over $460 million, with no control at the point just before orders left and 97 warning emails that nobody acted on ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)). The mechanism was unused code left reachable behind a flag that was later reused, and a deployment that missed one of eight servers with no second reviewer ([Varma](https://www.jrvarma.in/blog/Y2013/Knight-Capital.html)). Kaibot's retired intraday path, still running on the live loop, and its many environment switches are the same class of exposure at small scale. Alpaca also offers a free cross-check that Kaibot does not use: each fill event on its order stream carries the total position after the fill, though the documentation promises nothing about event order or replay after a reconnect ([Alpaca streaming](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/websocket-streaming)).

### Strategies that hold up are dull: diversify, cap volatility, trade rarely

Graded for a long-only, unborrowed account, the only robust improvement in risk-adjusted return is spreading across asset classes and capping portfolio volatility. A Stanford study of SPY, a bond fund, gold and cash over 2006 to 2026, long-only and monthly, found SPY alone at a Sharpe of 0.46 with a 55.2% worst drawdown, a 50/30/20 stock, bond and gold mix at 0.70 and 27.1%, and the same mix diluted with cash to a 7% volatility target at 0.82 and 15.9%, at returns roughly two to three points below SPY's 10.8%; the authors caution that twenty years leaves wide error bars ([Boyd et al.](https://web.stanford.edu/%7Eboyd/papers/pdf/stock_bond_gold_portfolios.pdf)). Volatility targeting without borrowing does not raise the Sharpe ratio in real time, 0.42 against 0.46 for the unmanaged market ([Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)); it shrinks drawdowns. Index trend filters are insurance: professional tactical-allocation funds earned about 5.0% a year over 20 years against 7.8% for a static 60/40 mix ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/funds/why-tactical-allocation-funds-failedagain)). The team's own calculation on 2017 to 2026 (monthly, no costs) agrees: a ten-month moving-average rule on SPY earned 7.8% a year against 13.7% for holding, a capped 15% volatility target earned 11.7% at an unchanged Sharpe with a quarter less drawdown, and 80% SPY with 20% gold matched SPY's return at lower risk, flattered by gold's recent run.

Two construction facts bear directly on Kaibot. A stricter rule to enter than to stay (buy in the top 10%, hold until out of the top 20%) cut momentum's monthly turnover from 34.5% to 18.8% and raised its net return ([Novy-Marx and Velikov](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)); `trend_momentum` enters and exits at the same 20% line every week. And the day a rule trades is a risk in itself: the same ten-month rule run on each of the 21 possible trading days of the month returned between 4.7% and 8.1% a year ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/tactical-asset-allocation-day-month-matter/)), and the authors of a 2026 re-test recommend splitting a book into tranches traded on different days ([Concretum](https://concretumgroup.com/global-tactical-asset-allocation/)). That is the evidence-based answer to buying on one fixed weekday: spread the decision over several days. Making buys news-driven is weaker ground, since news effects in large stocks are small and fading, as the AI section shows.

Costs set the speed limit. Retail market orders in S&P 500 stocks cost about 4.6 basis points per round trip in spread ([Huang, CQA slides](https://cqa.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Huang_Actual-Retail-Price_CQA.pdf)). By the team's arithmetic that is about 11.5% of capital a year for daily round trips, 2.4% weekly and 0.55% monthly, so only holding periods of weeks, or edges of 15 basis points or more a trade, are viable. The closing auction is the cheapest scheduled venue, with about half the price impact of continuous trading for large stocks ([Goyal, Jegadeesh and Wu](https://jfqa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/25551_Price_Impact.pdf)), and Alpaca accepts closing-auction orders only before 15:50 ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/orders-at-alpaca.md)). Alpaca's paper account runs no closing auction, however: on the first live session it filled 4 of 30 such buys in full and 3 in part, leaving about 16% of the book invested against a plan of about 90%, which is why Kaibot switched to market orders at 15:50 to 15:57 (`docs/prereg/trend_momentum.md`, Amendments 10 and 11). Research still prices those orders at the closing print, an open question the project's own decision record lists.

## What to build so the engine keeps finding its own edge cases

### Simulation with injected faults is the highest-leverage test, and Kaibot already owns the hard parts

Deterministic simulation testing runs the real engine in one process against simulated time, network and storage, with all randomness drawn from one seed, so any failure can be replayed exactly ([Antithesis](https://antithesis.com/docs/resources/deterministic_simulation_testing/)). TigerBeetle, a financial database, compresses months of operation into minutes this way while injecting network partitions, storage corruption and crashes, and checks invariants on internal state after every step ([TigerBeetle](https://tigerbeetle.com/blog/2026-08-20-protocol-aware-dst)). The method has known blind spots: an outside audit of TigerBeetle still found two safety issues and seven crashes, because the simulator's faults were too coarse, its generated workloads too regular, and upgrade and recovery paths under-tested ([Jepsen](https://jepsen.io/analyses/tigerbeetle-0.16.11)). Retrofitting simulation is normally impractical because every source of non-determinism must be pluggable; Kaibot already drives one core with a simulated broker and clock, so what remains (proposal) is a fault-injecting wrapper around `SimBroker`, a crash-and-restart loop, and invariant checks. Alpaca documents no ordering or redelivery guarantee for its fill stream, so the engine must be tested as if there were none.

The fault menu (proposal) should be fine-grained, following the Jepsen lesson: a submit that times out after Alpaca accepted it; a duplicated fill; a fill arriving before the acknowledgement or after a cancel request; a dropped event that only a later poll reveals; stale, missing, zero or crossed prices; a clock that jumps; a kill at each journal step; a Supabase write that fails, times out after succeeding, or arrives twice; and a watchdog that acts while the engine is merely paused. Nine invariants (proposal) are checked after every simulated event: cash plus positions equals equity to the cent; each broker execution is booked exactly once; at most one broker order exists per journalled intent across any number of restarts; order states move only along a legal graph and filled quantity never falls; risk caps hold counting working orders; no unintended short or negative cash; after faults stop the ledger equals the broker and no order stays unknown; journal sequence numbers are gapless and every send has a preceding intent; and no order leaves outside its window or uses data newer than "now".

Four cheaper techniques surround this. Hypothesis, a Python library, generates random sequences of actions against a state machine, checks invariants after every step and shrinks any failure to a short program that can be pasted into a test; its documentation's main example runs the real system beside a simple in-memory model and asserts they agree ([Hypothesis](https://hypothesis.readthedocs.io/en/latest/stateful.html)). For Kaibot the model is a hundred-line reference ledger (proposal). Crash testing by enumeration matters because crash bugs are common even in mature software: one study found 60 crash vulnerabilities across 11 widely used applications including SQLite and PostgreSQL ([ALICE, summarised](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/02/11/fs-not-equal/)). Mutation testing changes one line at a time and asks whether any test notices; across 13 Python projects with 83% to 99% line coverage it still found surviving mutants, at about 25 seconds per mutant ([PyTation, ICSE 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19088v1)), so it belongs on the money-critical modules, nightly. And because the engine is journal-first and deterministic, a nightly job can replay yesterday's journal through today's code and compare the decisions (proposal); the notes found no such feature documented for NautilusTrader or LEAN, which makes this a place where Kaibot can be better than the platforms it is measured against.

### Honest uses of AI and trained models: reviewer, proposer and veto, never trader

The evidence divides cleanly into what to build and what to leave. Machine learning for predicting stock returns is real but small. The benchmark study reports that its best models explain 0.3% to 0.7% of monthly return variation, with a gross long-short Sharpe of 1.35 when stocks are weighted by size against 2.45 when weighted equally ([Gu, Kelly and Xiu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)); follow-up work finds the profits concentrated in small, distressed and hard-to-trade stocks and reduced further by costs ([Avramov, Cheng and Metzker](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3450322)). On S&P 500 stocks over 2021 to 2024, a paper promoting its own agent method reports a rank correlation between forecast and outcome of only 0.0056 ([AlphaAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16789v2)). AQR, whose researchers co-wrote the benchmark study, calls the gains "real but modest" and points to risk modelling and trading costs as the better uses ([AQR](https://www.Aqr.Com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking-2Q19-Can-Machines-Learn-Finance.pdf?sc_lang=en)). The honest use (proposal) is a small gradient-boosted ranking model on daily price and volume features, with fixed pre-registered settings, used only to order candidates inside an existing rule and judged against random selection from the same candidates. Any backtest Sharpe above about 1 for such a model should be read as a data leak.

Language models reading news show a measurable effect that is small-cap, short-side and fading. One study after its model's knowledge cutoff found gross long-short Sharpe ratios near 3 on overnight headlines, stronger in small stocks, carried by the short leg, unprofitable at 20 basis points of round-trip cost, and falling from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 in early 2024 ([Lopez-Lira and Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). Backtesting a modern model on old text is contaminated: the same authors show a model recalling index levels and headline dates from before its cutoff, ignoring instructions to pretend otherwise, and naming the company behind anonymised earnings calls more than 85% of the time for every large technology firm tested ([Lopez-Lira, Tang and Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)); a separate test finds the contamination measurably inflates such results ([Gao, Jiang and Yan](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)). Researchers disagree on its size, with one team finding it modest for next-day news trades ([He et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.21206)). The safe reading is that it cannot be assumed away, so running today's model over Kaibot's 91,000 stored filings and backtesting the labels would break the project's point-in-time rule in spirit. The honest route is forward-only: keep the pre-open review in logging mode, pin the exact model version, record model, prompt and input for every call, and judge it only on dates after the model's cutoff and after the prompt was frozen. Under the project's rule that a language model may only veto or halve a trade, the testable hypothesis is a negative-news veto, which matches the finding that the short side carries the information. With a handful of planned buys a day, reaching the statistical bar may take a year or more, and the pre-registration should say so.

Reinforcement learning should not be built: in one evaluation by authors close to the field every agent lost money, and results swing with settings enough that a lucky agent can be picked ([Gort et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559)); a twenty-year independent benchmark found buy-and-hold among the best performers ([FINSABER](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v2)). "Agent" research systems report attractive numbers on short windows, mostly on Chinese indices, with best-of-N selection and no correction for the number of tries. The safe design (proposal) lets a model propose hypotheses in a restricted formula language over point-in-time fields, while deterministic code pre-registers, counts, tests and judges them, with a hard cap such as three new trials a week and a hurdle that rises with the count.

The best-evidenced AI use is in engineering. An Anthropic agent that writes Hypothesis tests produced 984 bug reports across more than 100 Python packages; on manual review 56% were valid, rising to 86% among the top-ranked, and fixes were merged into numpy and other libraries, though the first numpy fix proposed was wrong ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/property-based-testing)). That is a vendor's report on its own model with no independent replication. It supports a nightly job that proposes failing tests for a human to accept, never automatic patches to money-handling code. Cost is not the constraint. At prices fetched on 21 November 2026, Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, half that in batch ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)); the research team's arithmetic puts a daily pre-open review of ten planned buys at under a cent a day on that model and about 13 cents on Sonnet 5.5, so the $200 credit and the existing $1 a day cap cover every use above for well over a year. The nightly test-writing agent is the one item with no cost figure and needs its own cap.

### Six innovations that follow from the evidence

All six are the research team's proposals, each tied to a finding above. One: **standing targets**, a small ledger of what the latest decision still owes, so that late starts, missed windows and fail-safe cancels heal the same way. Two: **an evidence ladder for exits**, under which a forced sale of the whole book needs more proof than an entry (two independent price sources, fresh bars, no open reconciliation difference). Three: **the journal as a test oracle**, replaying each production day through the next release before it ships. Four: **a local liveness witness**, where the watchdog trusts the journal's last write time and the process table on the same disk before believing an unreadable cloud heartbeat, and the engine may start in a "reduce only" mode without the cloud. Five: **sleeve-on-core evaluation**, judging each rule as a deviation from an index core on its return above an exposure-matched SPY, with a family-wise correction, so that small real edges become visible and lucky ones do not. Six: **a counted AI proposer**, where every machine-suggested idea enters the same registry as a human one and raises the bar for all. None requires paid data, a larger server or leverage.

## Before a paid server, other users or app stores: law, entity and broker approval

### Automatic trading for others is licensed portfolio management

This subsection is research, not legal advice. BaFin states that automated portfolio management needs authorisation and that it does not apply only where the provider executes decisions the customer approves one by one; it describes the typical setup, a power over the customer's custody account without possession of funds, as still licensable ([BaFin](https://bafin.de/EN/Aufsicht/FinTech/Geschaeftsmodelle/BeratungsHandelssysteme/BeratungsHandelssysteme_node_en.html)). The EU securities regulator's non-binding briefing on copy trading says portfolio management applies where execution is automatic and needs no further client action, and remains likely even if clients can set leverage limits or stop-losses ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2023-03/ESMA35-42-1428_Supervisory_Briefing_on_Copy_Trading.pdf)). Managing for a single other person is enough, and contracts made without a licence are generally void with fees repayable ([FGS](https://www.fgs.de/news-and-insights/blog/detail/erlaubnispflichtiges-geschaeft-unterstuetzung-bei-der-vermoegensverwaltung-innerhalb-der-familie-und-im-engeren-bekanntenkreis)). Unlicensed provision is a criminal offence; an own licence limited to management, broking and advice without holding client money needs €75,000 of initial capital; and the cheaper "liability umbrella" route covers only broking, advice and placement, not portfolio management ([Bundesbank Merkblatt](https://bundesbank.de/resource/blob/895200/6a37b0634bb14fc8fb4b223560436f98/472B63F073F071307366337C94F8C870/merkblatt-wertpapierdienstleistungen-data.pdf)). One law-firm page gives €50,000; the Bundesbank figure is the more authoritative. Neither regulator has published a view on software the user configures and runs on their own keys. In the United States the exclusion that protects financial publishers was applied to portfolio alerts in 2024 by a court that expressly set auto-trading programmes apart ([Katten](https://katten.com/judge-dismisses-case-against-seeking-alpha-implications-for-publishers-of-financial-information)).

The researchers' inference is a three-stage path. Multi-user paper trading can be built now under ordinary privacy and consumer rules, with a lawyer confirming that a shipped stock-picking strategy is not a regulated investment recommendation. Live automation on users' own accounts must not launch without a written opinion from a German regulatory lawyer on the exact product flow, and may have to be redesigned around user-defined rules, per-trade confirmation, or software running in the user's own environment. An operator-run strategy on customers' accounts needs an own licence or a licensed partner as the contracting party. Excluding US persons at launch removes the US layer.

### Broker, data, tenancy and stores

Alpaca's Connect interface is the intended route: apps reach users' accounts through OAuth (a delegated login in which the app never sees the user's password or API secret), live trading for other users needs Alpaca's approval, and commercial apps need written approval ([Alpaca Connect](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/about-connect-api.md)). A grant can be restricted to paper, and most users can hold only one live data stream ([Alpaca OAuth](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/using-oauth2-and-trading-api)), so a server-side engine cannot assume it owns each user's price feed. The free data plan is IEX-only with 200 historical calls a minute; the full feed is $99 a month ([Alpaca market data](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/about-market-data-api.md)). A third-party tool states that Alpaca licenses data to the individual ([HawkAlpha](https://hawkalpha.gitbook.io/what-is-hawkalpha/alpaca-broker/regarding-market-data-licensing)), which, if Alpaca confirms it, means today's broadcast of the owner's live prices could not be shown to other users. Holding users' raw API keys is the pattern with a failure history: in December 2022 the chief executive of 3Commas confirmed that leaked user exchange keys were genuine ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/3commas-ceo-confirms-api-key-leak-following-warning-from-cz)).

For isolation, QuantConnect gives every live algorithm its own node, the smallest with half a gigabyte of memory ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/cloud-platform/live-trading/deployment.md)), and NautilusTrader supports one node per process ([NautilusTrader](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/architecture/)). Supabase's documented pattern is a tenant column on every table enforced by row-level security against a claim in the user's login token, with policies tested like code ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/kb/faqs/database/multi-tenant-isolation)); its Vault stores secrets encrypted with the key held outside the database, but the decrypted view must be locked down ([Supabase Vault](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/vault.md)). The researchers' proposal for one small server is a shared scheduler that computes signals once and then runs a short-lived, isolated worker per account; their capacity guess of 20 to 40 concurrent workers on two processor cores and 8 GB is an assumption to be replaced by a load test. The backlog already holds this as unbuilt, including "One isolated engine worker per customer" ([#88](https://github.com/falconomad/kaibot/issues/88)).

Apple's guidelines say trading apps should be submitted by the financial institution performing the service, with the necessary licences, and that apps in highly regulated fields should come from a legal entity and not an individual ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google Play requires an Organization account, with a D-U-N-S business number, for financial products such as stock trading ([Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10788890?hl=en)). A company is therefore a precondition for store apps, and a web app installable on the phone's home screen is the interim with no store risk (inference). Cross-border sales of digital services to EU consumers above €10,000 a year are taxed in the customer's country and can be reported through the One-Stop-Shop ([Accountable](https://www.accountable.de/en/help-center/one-stop-shop-oss-for-german-sole-proprietors-a-guide-for-freelancers-traders-and-small-business-owners/)). Privacy, consumer-terms and insurance points were not sourced and are a checklist for the lawyer.

### Decisions only the owner can take

| Decision | Why it is the owner's | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| Replace Gate B's absolute test with a sleeve test plus a book-level test (gate v3) | Gate marks change only by decision record | The current test cannot detect a small real edge; a correction for many tests must come with it |
| Hold SPY as a core at each level | A product and risk limit only the stakeholder confirms | Removes the largest single drag; the account then mostly is SPY |
| Which "better than SPY" is the goal | Sets every later pass mark | Less risk for similar return is supported; higher raw return is not |
| Allow non-stock diversifiers such as gold or managed-futures funds | Changes "US stocks" into a multi-asset product | The best-evidenced risk improvement, but decade-dependent |
| Keep leverage and short selling excluded | Standing project rule | They are the textbook ways to turn a higher Sharpe into a higher return; not researched further |
| Whether "veto or halve" also binds non-language-model machine learning | The rule was written for language models | A ranking overlay orders candidates, which is more than a veto |
| Replace the permanent book stop | Risk rule: tests first, two keys | Permanent retirement at 1.5 times one sample's drawdown adds path risk without a tested benefit |
| Remove the retired intraday rule from the live engine | Ends the daily soak test | It sits on the critical path of the rule that trades |
| A separate cap for index funds | Risk rule | The single-stock cap holds an SPY rule to 15% of the account |
| Freeze a hold-out window | Costs 18 to 24 months of test data | No final check exists today |
| Spend the one-time $125 Databento credit on spread calibration | Paid data is excluded by decision | One measurement settles the SPY cost question |
| Commission a German regulatory opinion, and when | Cost and legal exposure | Needed before any live multi-user step; the questions are drafted in the notes |
| Form a company | Cost and administration | Required by both app stores and for a commercial Alpaca app |
| Admit US users | Adds US adviser law | Excluding them at launch removes that layer |
| A second person with emergency halt access | Trust and privacy | A solo operator has no cover on holidays |

## Conclusion

The audit changes where the risk is understood to sit. Ten days ago the worry was the order path; that is now the strongest part of the system, and in write-ahead journaling it is stricter than the reference engine it was modelled on. The exposure has moved to time and evidence: whether a decision gets made and sent inside a seven-minute window, and how much proof the engine demands before its largest irreversible action. Both are cheap to fix and both are best fixed by one idea each, standing targets and an evidence ladder for exits, in place of more guarded special cases.

The second shift is in what "better than existing platforms" can honestly mean. It cannot mean returns, because the platforms do not supply returns and the evidence says a long-only, unborrowed book of large US stocks earns roughly the index minus its idle cash. It can mean an engine whose every production day is replayed through the next release, whose faults are searched for by seeded simulation every night, and whose research counts every attempt, including those a machine proposes. Those are things the open-source engines do not document and most retail products do not attempt. Three measurements would sharpen everything above and cost almost nothing: the paper account's real fill cost in SPY, the neighbour and exposure results already sitting on the Mac, and Alpaca's actual behaviour on a repeated order ID. The one item that engineering cannot settle is the legal route to other people's accounts, and that question should be put to a lawyer before the multi-user design is drawn, because the answer may decide where the engine has to run.

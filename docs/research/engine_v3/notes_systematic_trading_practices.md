# Systematic trading practices: how robust systems are designed, validated and run (notes for the Kaibot v3 rebuild)

Research date: 12 November 2026. Transferability tags used below: [T] transfers to a small, unlevered, long-only US equity account on free data; [P] partly transfers (principle yes, exact implementation no); [N] not transferable (needs leverage, futures, shorting or institutional infrastructure). Several primary papers were only reachable through abstracts or reputable secondary summaries (CXO Advisory, Alpha Architect, the7circles, Quantpedia); this is flagged where it matters.

## 1. Process: how do established firms and respected independent traders move from idea to live trading?

### Takeaway
The common pattern is: economic hypothesis first, a small number of pre-declared tests, out-of-sample or walk-forward evaluation done once, a paper/incubation period of 3 to 12 months, a small initial risk budget that is raised only after years of live evidence, and a pre-written rule for when a strategy stops trading. Kaibot's history (one rule, 17 months of data, straight to a live engine) skipped almost every one of these gates.

### Cited Findings

Robert Carver (ex-AHL, Systematic Trading, pysystemtrade)
- Carver prefers an "ideas first" route: begin with a plausible idea and calibrate only small variations; sifting thousands of variations for the few that look profitable is described as the road to overfitting. Calibration should target behaviour (trading speed, correlation with other rules), not returns. [T] — [the7circles summary of Systematic Trading, ch. 3-4](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- He fits on an expanding window (each year tested with rules fitted only on earlier years); fitting on the full sample flatters rules, a simple half/half split wastes data, and rolling windows need decades of data. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Data requirement by Sharpe ratio (two-sigma t-test): a rule with SR 0.5 needs about 10 years before its lower confidence band clears zero; SR 0.3 needs about 37 years; SR 1.0 only a few years; telling SR 0.3 apart from SR 0.8 takes about three decades. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Consequently he does not pick a best variation; he blends variations, uses the same generic rules on every instrument (pooling), and does not drop rules on historical performance (that data is reserved for setting forecast weights). [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Backtested Sharpe ratios are discounted by a "pessimism factor": roughly 65% to 70% of the backtested SR is carried forward; CXO's notes on the book give 0.75 for an out-of-sample bootstrap. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002); [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Backtested Sharpe ratios of 2 or 3 are called far too optimistic and a sign of overfitting; SR consistently above 1.0 is rarely achieved. His own system (about 45 instruments, eight rules, 30 variations) shows about SR 1.0 after costs in a 35-year backtest. — [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Capital ramp-up: a new trader should assume a low Sharpe (0.24, implying a 12% volatility target) and scale to 0.5 or more only after several years of strong live performance. [T] — [Carver, "How much risk should we take?"](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html)
- Do not override the system: in his April 2026 review he calls his own discretionary move to cash a mistake and "another failure of discretionary trading". [T] — [Carver, annual performance update year 12](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2026/04/annual-performance-update-year-12.html)

Kevin Davey (KJ Trading, "Strategy Factory")
- Sequence: (1) set objectives first (e.g. a return-to-drawdown target); (2) generate ideas, preferring simple, causal, longer-holding strategies, with first tests on small data subsets; (3) walk-forward optimisation run as a one-shot test with pre-defined pass criteria and no adjustment after seeing results; (4) Monte Carlo resampling of trades to see the range of drawdowns; (5) incubation, i.e. running live without money, for 6 to 12 months; (6) portfolio construction from a library; (7) monthly rotation removing poor performers. [T] — [Algo Advantage ep. 036](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/036-kevin-davey-part-i-its-all-about)
- Scale: library of over 200 strategies, about 20 deployed at once across 40 to 50 futures symbols, risk spread roughly equally over seven sectors. 85 to 90% of surveyed students credit incubation with catching bad strategies or confirming good ones. [P: futures] — [Algo Advantage ep. 036](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/036-kevin-davey-part-i-its-all-about)
- A second summary of his book gives incubation as three to six months, feasibility testing on one or two years of data, thousands of Monte Carlo runs, fixed-fractional sizing, and a pre-defined stop-trading threshold per strategy (example: stop after a $5,000 drawdown per contract). Conflict: incubation length is 3-6 months here versus 6-12 months in the podcast summary. — [FinancialWisdomTV summary](https://www.financialwisdomtv.com/post/building-winning-algorithmic-trading-systems-kevin-davey-s-complete-framework-for-trading-success)

Andreas Clenow (Stocks on the Move)
- The equity momentum system is published as a complete, mechanical rule set with a fixed weekly calendar (trade only on Wednesdays, resize every second Wednesday), an explicit universe (S&P 500), explicit disqualifiers and an index-level regime filter; nothing is left to judgment. Full rules are in section 5. [T] — [QuantConnect forum excerpt of the book rules](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/10493/andreas-clenow-momentum-strategy-using-framework-from-stocks-on-the-move-book/)

Ernest Chan
- Chan says he sticks to the simplest strategies because he lacks institutional infrastructure, and reports that at his earlier firms real-time results diverged from what the models and backtests showed. [T] — [Stocks & Commodities interview, 2009](https://www.traders.com/Documentation/FEEDbk_Docs/2009/07/Interview.html)

Perry Kaufman
- Kaufman runs a broad set of simple strategies to obtain an "average result", which he considers more likely to survive out of sample than a single backtested winner; he uses parameters tested over a wide range, few filters, and has migrated from shorter to longer-term trends over the years. He trades essentially the same strategies in futures and stocks, ranking and rotating stocks. [T] — [Algo Advantage interview with Perry Kaufman](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/a-wealth-of-experience-trading-diversified)

Institutional research protocols (AQR / Man / CFM / Research Affiliates-linked authors)
- Arnott, Harvey and Markowitz's seven-part backtesting protocol: (1) an ex-ante economic hypothesis, including asking who is on the other side of the trade; (2) keep count of every variable and combination tried; (3) no arbitrary data exclusion or cleaning, verify data accuracy; (4) recognise that true out-of-sample only means live trading or genuinely fresh data; (5) beware structural change and do not tweak a model on live results; (6) keep models simple; (7) reward sound process, not good results. [T] — [Alpha Architect summary of "A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning"](https://alphaarchitect.com/2018/12/protocol-to-prevent-quants-gone-wild)
- CFM (Rej, Seager, Bouchaud) model overfitting from iterative "improvements" and conclude that for typical CTA Sharpe ratios (0.3 to 0.5) backtest performance should be discounted by a factor of about 2, matching the industry rule of thumb; confirming an SR of 0.5 at 99.9% confidence would take about 43 years of data. [T] — [arXiv 1902.01802](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.01802)
- Harvey and Liu: the industry's customary 50% Sharpe haircut is too crude; the haircut should be non-linear (often above 50% for SR below 0.4, at most about 25% for SR above 1.0) and should depend on the number of strategies tried. [T] — [Harvey and Liu, "Backtesting"](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/dam/cmegroup/education/files/backtesting.pdf)
- Man AHL publishes its trend research as a blend rather than a pick: all trend speeds are run because they are lowly correlated, and "there is no perfect trend speed". [P] — [Man Institute, "The Need for Speed in Trend-Following Strategies"](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/need-for-speed-trend-following)

### Inferences
- A production-grade pipeline for Kaibot can be written as explicit gates, each with a pre-registered pass rule: hypothesis memo (who loses to us and why) -> fixed, small parameter grid logged in a trials register -> expanding-window or walk-forward test run once on 10+ years -> cost-stressed result -> Sharpe haircut of about 50% -> paper incubation of at least 3 to 6 months compared against backtest expectations -> small live allocation -> scale only after live tracking is inside the backtest's Monte Carlo band.
- There is a real disagreement on retirement: Davey rotates strategies monthly on performance and uses a hard per-strategy stop, whereas Carver and Arnott/Harvey/Markowitz warn against dropping or tweaking rules on recent results. A defensible synthesis is: never tune a live rule on live results, but pre-define a statistical retirement trigger (e.g. live drawdown beyond the Monte Carlo band) before go-live.
- Carver's data-requirement numbers imply that 17 months could not have validated any realistic rule: even a true SR of 1.0 needs "a few years", and realistic single-rule SRs of 0.3 to 0.5 need 10 to 37 years.

### Gaps
- No reliable public, specific statements on research process were found for Winton or Two Sigma in this session; AQR and Man are represented only through published papers by their researchers and advisers.
- Davey's exact pass thresholds (walk-forward efficiency, Monte Carlo risk-of-ruin limits, the commonly quoted "quit at 1.5x the Monte Carlo maximum drawdown" rule, idea-to-strategy hit rate) were not found in a citable source; they are in his books, which were not accessible.
- Chan's views on paper trading, Kelly sizing and strategy retirement were not in the accessible interview excerpt.
- pysystemtrade's specific production features (reconciliation, position limits, trade limits) were not researched.

## 2. Edge sources: what is realistically available to a small, unlevered, long-only account in liquid US equities, and what is not?

### Takeaway
What is available is slow, capacity-rich, well-documented premia (cross-sectional momentum, trend/regime filtering, volatility-managed exposure), harvested patiently and cheaply; what is not available is anything whose profit is compensation for providing liquidity or for speed. A dip-buying rule that enters with marketable orders pays the spread that the reversal premium exists to compensate, so it sits on the wrong side of the trade.

### Cited Findings

Available or plausibly available
- Momentum: AQR's "Fact, Fiction and Momentum Investing" (data from 1927, with extensions back to 1802 and across 40 countries) reports that momentum beat value and size in each sub-period tested, that the long side contributed slightly more than the short side over long periods, and that large-cap momentum returns are substantial though slightly lower than small-cap. The summary notes AQR's low cost estimates are institutional and do not directly help small investors. [T] — [Quant Investing summary of the AQR paper](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/10-myths-about-momentum-investing-squashed)
- Capacity is not the small trader's constraint for momentum: Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz, using about $1.7 trillion of live institutional trades (1998 to 2013), estimate a break-even fund size of $52 billion for momentum and real-world costs an order of magnitude below older academic estimates; they find size, value, momentum and short-term reversal survive costs, with short-term reversal the exception on capacity. [T for momentum] — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/surprise-the-size-value-and-momentum-anomalies-survive-after-trading-costs/)
- Volatility-managed equity exposure: because equity volatility rises when prices fall, scaling exposure down when volatility is high acts like a trend overlay and raised the US equity Sharpe ratio from 0.40 to about 0.48-0.51 in Man AHL/Duke research (1926 to 2017). [P: an unlevered account can only scale down from 100%] — [Alpha Architect summary of Harvey et al.](https://alphaarchitect.com/volatility-targeting-improves-risk-adjusted-returns/)
- Structural advantages of independents, per Chan: thinly traded and low-priced stocks that large funds cannot trade in size, infrequently trading strategies (monthly, quarterly, yearly) that funds needing constant profits ignore, and freedom from institutional constraints. [T] — [Stocks & Commodities interview](https://www.traders.com/Documentation/FEEDbk_Docs/2009/07/Interview.html)
- Kaufman notes lower commissions and better tools have levelled the field for retail traders, and he applies the same simple trend and ranking strategies to stocks. [T] — [Algo Advantage interview](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/a-wealth-of-experience-trading-diversified)

Decayed or doubtful
- Post-earnings-announcement drift: Martineau (Critical Finance Review, 2022) finds that for large stocks the drift has been absent since 2006 and has only recently vanished in microcaps, because prices now absorb earnings surprises on the announcement date. [Not a usable edge in liquid large caps] — [Critical Finance Review](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122)
- Published anomalies decay: across 97 published predictors, average returns were about 26% lower out of sample before publication and about 58% lower after publication, with the largest degradation three to four years after publication. — [CXO Advisory summary of McLean and Pontiff (2016)](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)

Not available (and why short-horizon reversal with liquidity-taking fails)
- Nagel's "Evaporating Liquidity": the return of short-term reversal strategies is a proxy for the return earned by liquidity providers; it is strongly time-varying, predictable with the VIX, and spikes in turmoil when liquidity supply is withdrawn. Reversal portfolios built from industries earn little on average and pay mainly when the VIX is high. — [NBER w17653](https://nber.org/papers/w17653)
- Quantpedia classifies reversal as usually a liquidity-providing strategy and a type of short-volatility position; it trades frequently in high-cost securities, so costs can prevent profitable execution. — [Quantpedia, short-term reversal in stocks](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/short-term-reversal-in-stocks)
- De Groot, Huij and Zhou: earlier studies found reversal profits disappear after trading costs; net profits of roughly 30 to 50 bps per week reappear only when the universe is restricted to the largest stocks and a turnover-reducing construction is used. This is a weekly, long-short, institutional-cost result (the Quantpedia example uses the 100 largest stocks, long 10 / short 10, about 0.29% per week net) and the result depends on which cost estimates are used. [N as specified: needs shorting, institutional execution] — [de Groot, Huij and Zhou (2012)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v36y2012i2p371-382.html); [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/short-term-reversal-in-stocks)
- Khandani and Lo: a simple simulated market-making (contrarian) strategy had significantly negative returns in the week of 6 August 2007 while being positive before and after, i.e. liquidity-provision strategies carry crash risk when market-making capital is withdrawn. — [NBER w14465](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14465)
- Speed-based edges: Carver's benchmark table puts top high-frequency traders at Sharpe 10+ and top hedge funds at 2 to 4, against about 1.0 for an experienced diversified retail systematic trader; QuantStart notes high-frequency profitability can turn on milliseconds of latency. [N] — [Carver](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html); [QuantStart Part II](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-II)
- Carver's cost framework rules out day trading altogether for retail cost levels (see section 3). — [the7circles summary, ch. on speed](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243)

### Inferences
- Why Kaibot's rule had zero gross edge and negative net edge: the reversal premium is the wage of whoever supplies liquidity (resting bids when others are forced to sell). Kaibot bought dips with marketable orders and exited with marketable orders at 15:55, so it paid the spread twice per trade instead of earning it, in the most liquid large caps where the reversal premium is smallest in calm markets (Nagel: little on average, paid mainly in high-VIX turmoil). The long-only constraint also removes the short leg that the academic reversal portfolios rely on.
- The intraday exit at 15:55 guarantees roughly 250 round trips per year per capital slot, which is the opposite of the low-turnover niches Chan identifies as the independent trader's advantage.
- Realistic candidate edges for v3 are therefore daily/weekly-bar, multi-week-holding rules: cross-sectional momentum ranking, an index trend/regime filter, and volatility-scaled exposure. Each should be haircut for post-publication decay (McLean-Pontiff: assume roughly half the published premium).
- "Structural" small-account edges (illiquid names) conflict with the brief's liquid large-cap universe and free data; they are noted but probably out of scope.

### Gaps
- No source was found that quantifies intraday (same-day) dip-buying in US large caps specifically; the evidence above is for daily-to-weekly reversal.
- Evidence on other candidate effects (overnight versus intraday return split, turn-of-month, low-volatility, quality) was not researched; a CXO teaser mentions a turn-of-month window averaging 0.084% daily S&P 500 return but without detail. — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)
- A December 2025 study teaser reports that liquidity constraints sharply cut Sharpe ratios across 128 US anomalies with fewer than 30% surviving a threshold; the teaser was truncated and the paper was not read. — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)
- AQR's exact momentum premium figures (annualised UMD return, long-side share) were in table images and could not be extracted.

## 3. Holding period versus cost: turnover, trading cost and required signal strength

### Takeaway
Carver's rule is the most usable one: express cost per round trip in Sharpe-ratio units, multiply by turnover, and never let annual cost exceed one third of a conservatively estimated pre-cost Sharpe ratio (in practice a cap of about 0.13 SR per year). Under any plausible cost assumption a once-a-day round trip needs a pre-cost Sharpe far above what single rules deliver, which is why practitioners who lack an execution edge hold for weeks to months.

### Cited Findings
- Standardised cost = (cost to buy + cost to sell) / annualised volatility of the instrument, with annualised volatility taken as 16 x daily volatility. Turnover = round trips of the average position per year (turnover 26 = two-week holding period). Annual cost in SR units = round-trip cost x turnover. — [the7circles summary of Carver](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243)
- Speed limit: costs must be no more than one third of the pre-cost Sharpe ratio; since most single trading subsystems have SR below 0.4, this is a ceiling of 0.13 SR per year. At a 25% volatility target that is about 3.35% a year in costs. Rules costing more than 0.13 SR a year are excluded. — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243); [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Worked example: an instrument costing 0.002 SR per round trip allows about 65 round trips a year, a holding period of about four trading days; futures cost 0.001 to 0.030 SR, ETFs are assumed at 0.08 SR. The summary lists minimum holding periods of about one month for futures, at least six months for ETFs and upwards of 2.5 years for individual stocks, and states day trading is ruled out. — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243)
- Faster variations need considerable evidence to justify them over slower, cheaper ones. — [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- A professional-grade independent's actual cost: Carver's futures account paid 0.88% all-in for the year to April 2026 (0.55% slippage plus 0.33% commission), in line with earlier years, against a long-run mean return of 13.8% at 17.3% volatility; his execution algorithm cut slippage by about 40% (1.34% to 0.55%). — [Carver, annual update year 12](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2026/04/annual-performance-update-year-12.html)
- Man AHL (50 futures and FX markets, 1995 to August 2022): pre-cost risk-adjusted returns rise as trend speed slows; costs hit fast speeds hardest because of turnover and more spread crossing, and after costs the Sharpe of fast models declines materially. Fast models keep one virtue, better returns in the worst equity quintile. Man says its own execution halves costs versus bank algorithms. [P] — [Man Institute](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/need-for-speed-trend-following)
- Momentum has 5 to 6 times the turnover of value yet survives institutional costs. — [Quant Investing summary of AQR](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/10-myths-about-momentum-investing-squashed)
- Real institutional costs for broad exposure were about 6 bps a year for the S&P 500 and 39 bps for the CRSP value-weighted index, versus 1.39% and 5.28% implied by older academic cost models. — [Alpha Architect summary of Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz](https://alphaarchitect.com/surprise-the-size-value-and-momentum-anomalies-survive-after-trading-costs/)
- Grinold's fundamental law: IR = IC x sqrt(breadth); with constraints, IR = TC x IC x sqrt(breadth), where IC is the correlation of forecasts with outcomes, breadth is the number of independent bets per year, and the transfer coefficient TC falls below 1 when constraints such as a short-selling ban, sector limits or turnover limits bind. The textbook example (monthly IC 0.04 on 1,050 stocks giving IR 4.49) is called unrealistically high, which is why TC is needed. — [AnalystPrep, fundamental law](https://analystprep.com/study-notes/cfa-level-2/state-and-interpret-the-fundamental-law-of-active-portfolio-management-including-its-component-terms-transfer-coefficient-information-coefficient-breadth-and-active-risk-aggressiveness/)
- Backtests that ignore or underestimate costs are a standard beginner error; slippage depends on volatility, latency and strategy type, with momentum systems suffering more slippage on average than mean-reverting ones; flat fixed-cost models are rarely adequate. — [QuantStart Part II](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-II)

### Inferences
- Applying Carver's arithmetic to Kaibot's old rule (my calculation; the cost and volatility inputs are assumptions, not sourced measurements): for a large-cap stock with 30% annualised volatility, a 5 bps round-trip cost is 0.0017 SR per trade. At 250 round trips a year that is about 0.42 SR of annual cost, so the one-third rule would require a pre-cost SR of about 1.25 from a single rule. At 3 bps it is 0.25 SR (requires 0.75); at 10 bps it is 0.83 SR (requires 2.5). The ten-year test showed a pre-cost SR of about zero, so every trade was expected to lose the cost.
- Inverting it: with a 0.13 SR budget and the same assumptions, the fastest permissible turnover is about 40 to 130 round trips a year (holding roughly 2 to 6 trading days) and that only if the rule truly has SR 0.4; a rule with SR 0.2 gets half that budget. Weekly-to-monthly holding is the natural zone, consistent with Clenow's weekly schedule.
- Breakeven cost analysis for any candidate rule: gross expected return per trade must exceed round-trip cost by a factor of at least 3 (the one-third rule restated per trade). This should be a hard gate in the v3 validation pipeline, computed with Kaibot's own measured paper/live slippage rather than assumed zero-commission costs.
- The fundamental law explains why "many trades" did not help: 250 daily dip-buys in correlated large caps are not 250 independent bets, the long-only constraint lowers TC, and with IC near zero the product is zero regardless of breadth.
- The "2.5 years for individual stocks" figure from the summary of Carver's book reflects that book's retail cost inputs for shares, which the summary does not spell out; it should be recomputed for a zero-commission US broker rather than adopted literally. The method, not the number, transfers.

### Gaps
- No sourced measurement of actual effective spreads/slippage for retail marketable orders at Alpaca was collected; the 3 to 10 bps range above is an assumption to be replaced with measured data.
- No source was found giving an alternative numeric rule of thumb (other than Carver's one third) for the maximum share of expected return that costs may consume.
- Man AHL's Sharpe ratios by trend speed were in a graphic that did not load.

## 4. Diversification across strategies and instruments

### Takeaway
Practitioners do not look for the best rule; they average several simple, weakly correlated rules (and several speeds of each) across as many instruments as they can, because historical data almost never proves one variation superior. Typical counts are 2 to 8 rule families, 4 to 5 speeds for momentum, and tens of instruments or strategies; correlation between return streams, not the count, is what matters.

### Cited Findings
- Carver: a balanced mix of rules from different styles beats any single rule; forecasts are combined as a weighted average and rescaled by a forecast diversification multiplier, and individual forecasts are capped. — [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Carver's own configuration: about 45 instruments, eight trading rules and 30 variations (CXO says four styles; the7circles says five themes), SR about 1.0 after costs over a 35-year backtest. He suggests beginners start with two rules, trend following and carry. [P: carry and 45 futures markets need futures] — [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading); [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Momentum needs four or five variations at different speeds; pairs of variations correlated 0.95 or more are pruned to one; diversifying across instruments matters more than adding variations, because variations on one instrument are highly correlated; any rule with a positive SR may help depending on its correlation with the rest, and post-haircut SRs above 0.2 are very likely useful. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Weights: "handcrafting" assumes equal volatility and equal SR so that only correlations drive weights (group similar things, weight within and across groups); formal optimisation tends to overfit. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Man AHL: the fastest and slowest trend speeds correlate at only 0.17, so all speeds are run together. [P] — [Man Institute](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/need-for-speed-trend-following)
- Davey: about 20 strategies live from a library of 200+, equal risk across seven sectors. [P] — [Algo Advantage ep. 036](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/036-kevin-davey-part-i-its-all-about)
- Kaufman: layers many simple strategies to get the average result; notes the trade-off that more markets lower both risk and return. [T] — [Algo Advantage interview](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/a-wealth-of-experience-trading-diversified)
- AQR: a 60% value / 40% momentum mix had the highest share of positive rolling 1- and 5-year periods and the best Sharpe; in 1999 value did terribly while momentum did well, and in 2009 the reverse. [T] — [Quant Investing summary of AQR](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/10-myths-about-momentum-investing-squashed)
- Quantopian cohort: an equal-weighted portfolio of 10 strategies chosen by a model using many in-sample features reached an out-of-sample Sharpe of 1.8 versus 0.7 for the top 10 chosen by in-sample Sharpe. — [CXO Advisory summary of Wiecki et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/in-sample-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-888-trading-strategies)
- Limits of diversification: constituents of the SG CTA Index have an average pairwise correlation of about 50%; in Carver's year to April 2025 almost none of his trading rules made money (his worst futures year, -16.3%). — [CFM](https://www.cfm.com/steady-trends-the-reality-of-cta-return-dispersion/); [Carver, annual update year 12](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2026/04/annual-performance-update-year-12.html)

### Inferences
- For a long-only US equity engine the transferable form is: several rule families with different economic rationales (e.g. cross-sectional momentum, index trend/regime, volatility-managed exposure, possibly a value or quality tilt), each run at several lookbacks, combined by fixed handcrafted weights, across many stocks and sectors. The cross-asset diversification that drives CTA Sharpe ratios (bonds, commodities, FX, shorting) is not available, so expected Sharpe improvement from combination is smaller than Carver's 0.4-per-rule to 1.0-combined example.
- All long-only equity rules share market beta, so their correlation will be far higher than Man AHL's 0.17 between speeds; diversification benefit should be measured on returns in excess of the index, not on raw returns.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that directly compares "several simple combined rules" against "one optimised rule" out of sample for long-only equities; the evidence above is practitioner design choices plus Carver's statistical argument.
- Numbers for forecast diversification multipliers and handcrafted weight tables were not extracted.

## 5. Position sizing and portfolio construction

### Takeaway
Size by risk, not by conviction or dollars: target a portfolio volatility chosen from a conservative Sharpe estimate (half-Kelly), give each position an equal slice of risk via inverse-volatility sizing, and rebalance on a slow, buffered schedule. Typical numbers are a 10 to 25% annual volatility target for the portfolio and about 10 bps of equity in daily volatility per stock position.

### Cited Findings
- Kelly link: optimal volatility target equals expected Sharpe ratio (SR 0.5 implies 50% volatility at full Kelly); Carver recommends half of that (SR 0.5 implies 25%). Choose the most conservative of: risk appetite, broker leverage limit, fat-tail-safe leverage, and the Kelly figure. — [Carver, "How much risk should we take?"](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html)
- Reference targets: 12% for a simple one-rule, one-instrument system; most professional managers 10 to 30%; Carver's own 25% (the book's maximum advocated is 37%); stock-like volatility is about 15 to 25% a year (about 16% for the S&P 500). Implied targets of 100%+ promoted by online "gurus" are called madness. — [Carver](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html); [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Realised versus target: Carver's live account has realised 17.3% annualised volatility against the 25% target. — [Carver, annual update year 12](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2026/04/annual-performance-update-year-12.html)
- Volatility estimate and trading buffer: price volatility from a 25-day EMA (longer than 100 days does worse); do not trade when a position is within 10% of its target. [T] — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243); [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Carver's test of dynamic position volatility scaling versus near-fixed scaling on his trend system: Sharpe 0.92 versus 0.57 on full data (0.78 versus 0.52 excluding pre-1981), kurtosis 5.3 versus 33; dropping volatility scaling costs about a third of the Sharpe ratio. [P: futures] — [Carver, "Vol targeting and trend following"](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2018/07/vol-targeting-and-trend-following.html)
- Man AHL/Duke volatility targeting (60 assets, 1926 to 2017, 10% target): improves Sharpe for equities and credit and for 60/40 and risk-parity portfolios, not for commodities; cuts both tails; US equity volatility-of-volatility fell from 4.6% to 1.8%; maximum drawdowns of balanced portfolios fell. [P: full benefit needs leverage in quiet periods] — [Alpha Architect summary of Harvey et al.](https://alphaarchitect.com/volatility-targeting-improves-risk-adjusted-returns/)
- Clenow's long-only equity rules (the closest published template to Kaibot's constraints) [T]:
  - Universe S&P 500; rank by annualised slope of a 90-day exponential regression multiplied by its R-squared.
  - Disqualify a stock if it is below its 100-day moving average or had a move/gap above 15% in the last 90 days.
  - Position size: shares = account value x 0.001 / ATR(20), i.e. each position targets a daily move of 10 bps of equity (naive risk parity).
  - Buy only when the S&P 500 is above its 200-day moving average; fill from the top of the ranking until cash runs out; typically 10 to 30 positions.
  - Sell when a stock leaves the top 20% of the ranking, falls below its 100-day average, gaps more than 15%, or leaves the index; check weekly (Wednesdays), resize every second week and only when the deviation is significant.
  - One implementer puts the minimum account at $30,000 and reports volatility well below the index and good behaviour in 2008. — [QuantConnect forum excerpt](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/10493/andreas-clenow-momentum-strategy-using-framework-from-stocks-on-the-move-book/); [TuringTrader](https://turingtrader-com.web.app/portfolios/clenow-stocks-on-the-move)
- Davey uses fixed-fractional sizing and equal risk per sector; Kaufman takes top-ranked stocks "all in" and prefers fewer markets with more risk. — [FinancialWisdomTV](https://www.financialwisdomtv.com/post/building-winning-algorithmic-trading-systems-kevin-davey-s-complete-framework-for-trading-success); [Algo Advantage interview](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/a-wealth-of-experience-trading-diversified)
- Leverage is where sizing errors become fatal: LTCM ran about 25:1 balance-sheet leverage (about $4.7 billion equity against about $125 billion assets, over $1 trillion derivatives notional) and lost $4.6 billion in under four months in 1998. [N for Kaibot, which is unlevered] — [Quantt, LTCM](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/long-term-capital-management-collapse)

### Inferences
- For an unlevered long-only book, "volatility targeting" reduces to an exposure dial between 0% and 100%: when estimated portfolio volatility exceeds the target, hold cash; it cannot lever up in calm markets, so the Sharpe gain will be smaller than in the leveraged studies but the tail-trimming effect should carry over.
- With Carver's half-Kelly logic and a conservative excess-return SR of about 0.3 to 0.5 for a long-only equity system, the Kelly-implied ceiling is 15 to 25% annual volatility, i.e. roughly "fully invested stock-like risk" at most. There is no sizing argument for concentrating beyond that at this account size.
- Clenow's 10 bps daily risk per position is about 1.6% annualised volatility contribution per position (0.1% x 16); 20 such positions that were perfectly correlated would give about 2% daily portfolio volatility, so in practice the count of positions is set by cash, not by the risk unit.
- At $5,000 the Clenow-style equal-risk book is hard to hold without fractional shares (TuringTrader's $30,000 minimum); fractional-share support or a smaller universe of positions would be needed at the low end of Kaibot's account range.
- Gap risk is handled in the cited practice by screening and sizing (exclude names with recent 15% moves; size by ATR), not by relying on stop orders.

### Gaps
- No sourced numeric norms were found for per-position or per-sector caps in long-only systematic equity books; Clenow's rules imply caps only indirectly.
- Equal risk contribution / risk parity with a full covariance matrix was not researched beyond the naive inverse-volatility form.
- No source addressed overnight-gap handling for stop orders specifically.

## 6. Risk management and drawdown control

### Takeaway
Stop-losses are not a free risk reducer: theory and evidence say they lower expected return under random-walk or mean-reverting returns and help only when returns trend or switch regimes, and only at longer horizons with wide thresholds. Robust systems control drawdowns mainly through sizing, diversification, slow regime filters and automated portfolio-level limits.

### Cited Findings
- Kaminski and Lo, theory: under a random walk a stop-loss always lowers expected return; under mean reversion it hurts; under positive serial correlation (momentum) it can add value once autocorrelation is large relative to the Sharpe ratio at that frequency; under regime switching it helps only if the rule identifies the bad regime accurately enough. Switching to a lower-volatility asset when stopped always lowers variance. — [Kaminski and Lo (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/114876/Lo_When%20Do%20Stop-Loss.pdf)
- Kaminski and Lo, evidence (daily S&P and Treasury futures, January 1993 to November 2011): short-horizon stop policies (daily windows) had negative premiums over most parameters; policies defined over horizons longer than a month could be positive, and one monthly calibration raised return by about 1.5%, cut volatility by about 5% and lifted Sharpe by up to 20%. The published abstract says the same: benefit appears at longer sampling frequencies. — [Kaminski and Lo (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/114876/Lo_When%20Do%20Stop-Loss.pdf); [Journal of Financial Markets abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:234-254)
- Earlier working-paper version (US stocks 1950 to 2004, 10% stop into long-term bonds, re-enter on recovery): stopped-out periods beat bonds only about 30% of the time. — [Quant Investing summary](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/truths-about-stop-losses-that-nobody-wants-to-believe)
- Tight stops are the worst: on OMX Stockholm 30 stocks (1998 to 2009) a 5% trailing stop was the only level that underperformed buy and hold; 15 to 20% trailing stops did best. — [Quant Investing summary of Snorrason and Yusupov](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/truths-about-stop-losses-that-nobody-wants-to-believe)
- Stops help trend/momentum strategies: Han, Zhou and Zhu (US stocks 1926 to 2011) add a 10% stop to monthly long-short momentum; worst month improves from -49.79% to -11.34%, mean monthly return rises from 1.01% to 1.73%, Sharpe from 0.166 to 0.371 (equal-weighted). [P: long-short] — [Quant Investing summary](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/truths-about-stop-losses-that-nobody-wants-to-believe)
- Carver: exits by systematic trailing stop are acceptable, but stop tightness determines holding period and therefore cost; wider stops permit more expensive instruments; do not change a forecast after a position is open. — [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=24243); [CXO Advisory notes](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/a-few-notes-on-systematic-trading)
- Kaufman avoids individual stop-losses and instead exits on portfolio-level "extreme volatility" measures. [T] — [Algo Advantage interview](https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/a-wealth-of-experience-trading-diversified)
- Regime filters in practice: Clenow blocks new buys when the S&P 500 is below its 200-day average. A monthly stock/bond rule using 11-month trend and recent volatility had a worst peak-to-trough fall of 26% versus 62% for 60/40 over 1926 to 2020. [T] — [QuantConnect forum excerpt](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/10493/andreas-clenow-momentum-strategy-using-framework-from-stocks-on-the-move-book/); [Quant Investing summary of Samonov and Sorokina](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/truths-about-stop-losses-that-nobody-wants-to-believe)
- Davey sets a stop-trading threshold for each strategy before it goes live. [T] — [FinancialWisdomTV](https://www.financialwisdomtv.com/post/building-winning-algorithmic-trading-systems-kevin-davey-s-complete-framework-for-trading-success)
- Drawdowns are normal even for the best-known systematic style: the SG Trend Index has had 16 drawdowns above 10% since 2000 (about one every 18 to 24 months) and fell 20.4% from May 2024 to May 2025. — [Cambridge Associates, August 2025](https://publishedresearch.cambridgeassociates.com/?p=19140)
- Human tolerance is a failure mode: backtested drawdowns look tolerable, live ones lead traders to switch strategies off at the worst time (example given: a 25% drawdown lasting four months). — [QuantStart Part I](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-I/)
- Automated limits matter more than monitoring by humans: Knight's main risk tool was post-execution, human-monitored, generated no automatic alerts and was not linked to order entry (section 8). — [SEC order, Release 70694](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/kcg-holdings-inc-18985/contract/555902)

### Inferences
- Kaibot's old rule combined a mean-reversion entry (buy the dip) with a tight stop. By Kaminski and Lo's framework this is the adverse pairing: a strategy that bets on reversal is stopped out precisely when the reversal bet is at its best expected value, and the tight stop raises turnover and cost. Either the premise (mean reversion) or the stop was wrong; they cannot both be right.
- For v3: use wide, slow exits consistent with the signal's logic (rank-based or trend-based exits for momentum), put drawdown control at the portfolio level (volatility-scaled exposure, index regime filter, a pre-declared de-risking schedule), and keep a hard automated kill switch for operational failures rather than for market noise.
- Strategy-level "quit" rules should be statistical (live drawdown or underperformance outside the backtest's simulated distribution), declared before go-live.

### Gaps
- No specific, sourced numeric conventions were found for portfolio-level drawdown stops (e.g. "cut risk by half at -X%") among the named practitioners.
- The 1950-2004 results of Kaminski and Lo are reported from a secondary blog summary of the working paper; the published version uses the 1993-2011 futures sample.
- Evidence on stop-losses for long-only cross-sectional equity momentum specifically was not found (Han et al. is long-short).

## 7. Validation and overfitting

### Takeaway
A backtest is evidence only in proportion to its length and in inverse proportion to the number of things tried. The working standards are: economic rationale first, a t-statistic near 3 rather than 2, a log of all trials, a Sharpe haircut of roughly half, minimum backtest lengths measured in decades for realistic Sharpe ratios, and leakage-free cross-validation; even then, backtest Sharpe barely predicts live Sharpe.

### Cited Findings
- Harvey, Liu and Zhu: after hundreds of published factors, a newly discovered factor should clear a t-ratio of about 3.0 rather than 2.0; the authors conclude most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false. — [NBER w20592](https://nber.org/papers/w20592)
- Harvey and Liu worked example: 20 years of monthly data with SR 0.75 has a single-test p-value of about 0.0008; if 200 strategies were tried, the adjusted p-value is about 0.15 and the haircut SR about 0.32 (a 60% cut). With 300 tests, the required average monthly return at 10% volatility rises from about 0.365% to about 0.62% under the BHY adjustment. Procedure: convert SR to a t-ratio, adjust the p-value for the number of tests (Bonferroni, Holm or BHY), convert back. — [Harvey and Liu, "Backtesting"](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/dam/cmegroup/education/files/backtesting.pdf)
- Minimum backtest length (Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado, Zhu): MinBTL (years) is approximately [((1 - g) Z^-1(1 - 1/N) + g Z^-1(1 - 1/(N e))) / target SR]^2, bounded above by 2 ln(N) / SR^2, with g the Euler-Mascheroni constant and N the number of independent trials. With 5 years of data, no more than 45 independent configurations can be tried before a skill-less in-sample SR of 1 is expected. Analysts rarely report N, so overfitting cannot be assessed. — [Bailey et al., "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism"](https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf)
- Same paper: a hold-out set does not solve the problem, because it ignores how many trials preceded selection and the researcher has usually seen the hold-out; when returns have memory, overfit strategies tend to lose money out of sample rather than merely earn zero. — [Bailey et al.](https://carmamaths.org/jon/backtest.pdf); [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/insidiousness-of-overfitting-investment-strategies-via-iterative-backtests)
- Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey and Lopez de Prado): corrects a reported Sharpe for selection bias under multiple testing and for non-normal returns, to separate real findings from flukes; related tools are Minimum Track Record Length and Minimum Backtest Length. — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)
- Purged and combinatorial cross-validation (Lopez de Prado): purging removes training observations whose label windows overlap the test set; an embargo drops a further buffer after each test fold (e.g. 5% of 1,000 observations = 50); CPCV splits the series into N groups and tests every combination of k groups (N=6, k=2 gives 15 splits and 5 full backtest paths), yielding a distribution of Sharpe ratios rather than the single path of walk-forward, at high computational cost. — [Wikipedia, purged cross-validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation)
- Backtests barely predict live results: across 888 Quantopian algorithms (in-sample 2010 to deployment in 2015, out-of-sample June 2015 to February 2016), in-sample metrics explained 1 to 2% of out-of-sample performance; volatility (R-squared 0.67) and maximum drawdown (0.34) were predictable, returns were not; the more backtests a developer had run, the larger the in-sample to out-of-sample gap. Caveat: a short, calm out-of-sample window. — [CXO Advisory summary of Wiecki et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/in-sample-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-888-trading-strategies)
- Even honest, published results decay: -26% out of sample, -58% post-publication (97 predictors). — [CXO Advisory summary of McLean and Pontiff](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)
- CFM's overfitting model: discount by about 2x for SR 0.3 to 0.5; a higher acceptance bar and more freedom to tweak both increase overfitting. — [arXiv 1902.01802](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.01802)
- Role of economic rationale: start from an ex-ante hypothesis and ask who is on the other side (Arnott, Harvey, Markowitz); start from a plausible idea, not a data sweep (Carver). — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2018/12/protocol-to-prevent-quants-gone-wild); [the7circles](https://the7circles.uk/?p=29002)
- Common backtest errors with concrete examples:
  - Look-ahead: array/index offset bugs; fitting regression coefficients or normalisation statistics on the full sample and applying them to earlier trades; using a bar's high or low before the bar has closed (remedy: lag by at least one period). — [QuantStart Part I](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-I/)
  - Survivorship: testing through the 2001 crash using only tech stocks that survived; Yahoo Finance data is not survivorship-free; remedies are bias-free datasets or collecting your own point-in-time data going forward. Clenow's sell rule "has left the index" presupposes point-in-time index membership. — [QuantStart Part I](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-I/); [QuantConnect forum excerpt](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/10493/andreas-clenow-momentum-strategy-using-framework-from-stocks-on-the-move-book/)
  - Unrealistic fills: free daily OHLC is often a composite of several exchanges, so highs and lows can come from small regional prints or uncorrected tick errors and are not obtainable; market orders fill across the book at uncertain prices; limit orders may fill partially or not at all; short-horizon backtests can materially outperform live trading when impact and the book are poorly modelled. — [QuantStart Part II](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-II)
  - Optimisation bias: check the parameter "surface"; a smooth plateau suggests a real effect, a jagged one an artefact. — [QuantStart Part I](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-I/)
  - Bad data: a dataset in which market capitalisation exceeded $10 trillion for 20% of observations. — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2018/12/protocol-to-prevent-quants-gone-wild)

### Inferences
- Applying the MinBTL formula to Kaibot's original validation (my calculation from the paper's formula): 17 months is 1.42 years, which is the minimum backtest length for only about 5 independent configurations. In other words, trying five variants of stop, target and dip threshold on 17 months is expected to produce an in-sample annualised Sharpe of 1.0 with no skill at all; 10 variants need about 2.5 years, 100 need about 6.4 years, 1,000 about 10.6 years.
- Standard error of an annualised Sharpe is roughly 1/sqrt(years): about 0.84 at 17 months, 0.32 at 10 years. A 17-month SR would have to exceed about 1.7 to be two standard errors from zero before any multiple-testing adjustment; a t-stat of 3 at SR 0.5 needs about 36 years, which is why practitioners pool across many instruments and lean on economic rationale instead of waiting for significance on a single rule.
- A minimum v3 validation standard that a small team can implement: (a) written hypothesis; (b) trials register with every configuration ever run; (c) at least 10 years, ideally 15 to 20, of survivorship-free daily data including a bear market; (d) expanding-window or walk-forward as the baseline and CPCV if labels overlap; (e) report deflated or haircut Sharpe, not raw; (f) parameter-plateau check; (g) cost stress at 2x measured costs; (h) paper incubation with pre-declared tolerance bands.
- Free data is the binding constraint for (c): survivorship-free constituent history is usually not free, so either restrict to instruments where survivorship is immaterial (ETFs, current mega caps with an explicit bias allowance) or start recording point-in-time universe data now.

### Gaps
- The probability of backtest overfitting (PBO, via combinatorially symmetric cross-validation) is not defined in any source fetched this session. From background knowledge, not verified here: PBO is the estimated probability that the configuration ranked best in-sample ranks below the median out of sample; the report writer should cite Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu, "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting", directly if this is used.
- The DSR formula inputs (number of trials, variance of trial Sharpe ratios, skewness, kurtosis, sample length) were not confirmed from the abstract page; they are from background knowledge.
- No sourced figure was found for the size of survivorship bias in US large-cap backtests (e.g. percentage points per year).
- Wiecki et al. and McLean-Pontiff were read through CXO Advisory summaries, not the papers.

## 8. Documented failures and their lessons for engine design

### Takeaway
The large failures were operational (Knight), crowding/liquidity (August 2007) and leverage (LTCM); the common small-trader failure is statistical (overfit strategies that die live, costs ignored). For an unlevered paper engine the directly transferable lessons are Knight's: controlled deployments, no dead code behind reused flags, automated pre-trade limits, a kill switch and an incident runbook.

### Cited Findings

Knight Capital, 1 August 2012
- In the first 45 minutes of trading Knight's router sent more than 4 million orders while trying to fill 212 customer orders, traded more than 397 million shares, built several billion dollars of unwanted positions and lost more than $460 million; the SEC fined it $12 million under the market access rule. — [SEC press release 2013-222](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)
- Positions reached about $3.5 billion net long in 80 stocks and about $3.15 billion net short in 74 stocks. — [SEC order, Release 70694](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/kcg-holdings-inc-18985/contract/555902)
- Root cause chain: "Power Peg" code retired in 2003 was left on servers behind a flag; a 2005 change moved the cumulative-fill check without retesting the dormant code; in 2012 the flag was repurposed for new Retail Liquidity Program code; one technician failed to copy the new code to the eighth of eight servers and no second person reviewed the deployment; orders with the reused flag hit the stale server, which kept sending child orders without counting fills. — [J. R. Varma's reading of the SEC order](https://www.jrvarma.in/blog/Y2013/Knight-Capital.html)
- Missed warnings and missing controls: 97 automated emails referencing the router and the error "Power Peg disabled" were sent before the open and not acted on; no written deployment procedure or peer review; the primary risk monitor (PMON) was post-trade, human-watched, had no automated alerts and was not linked to order entry; the account accumulating the positions had a $2 million limit that was not connected to firm-wide automated controls; no procedure existed to halt the router; with no incident procedure, staff uninstalled the new code from the seven correct servers, which activated the bad code there too. — [SEC order, Release 70694](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/kcg-holdings-inc-18985/contract/555902); [SEC press release](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)

August 2007 quant quake
- In the week of 6 August 2007 several quantitative long/short equity funds suffered unprecedented losses; Khandani and Lo attribute it to the rapid unwind of one or more large market-neutral portfolios (probably a forced liquidation by a multi-strategy fund or prop desk), which pushed losses onto similarly positioned long/short and long-only portfolios and triggered their stop-loss and de-leveraging rules; strategies rebounded sharply on 10 August. The shock originated outside equities. — [Khandani and Lo (2007) abstract](https://web.mit.edu/~alo/www/Papers/august07.html)
- Follow-up with transactions data: the unwind began in July; a simple market-making strategy lost significantly that week; a mini-unwind occurred on 1 August (10:45 to 11:30) and a sustained one from the open on 6 August, starting in financials, long book-to-market and short earnings momentum; market-making capital was temporarily withdrawn from 8 August. — [NBER w14465](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14465)

LTCM, 1998
- Net returns of 20% (1994, partial), 43%, 41% and 17%; about 25:1 leverage and over $1 trillion derivatives notional; after Russia's default in August 1998 it lost more than $100 million a day, $4.6 billion in under four months, and was recapitalised with $3.6 billion from its bank counterparties under the New York Fed on 23 September 1998. Lessons listed: leverage magnifies unmodelled risk, correlations in stress differ from normal times, liquidity is conditional, positions were too large to exit, models assumed continuous liquidity. — [Quantt, LTCM](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/long-term-capital-management-collapse)

Retail and crowd-sourced algo failure patterns
- Quantopian's 888-algorithm cohort: backtest performance essentially did not predict live performance, annual returns were slightly negatively related in- versus out-of-sample (developers overfit return and ignored volatility), and heavier backtesting meant a bigger live shortfall. — [CXO Advisory summary of Wiecki et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/in-sample-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-888-trading-strategies)
- Carver's benchmark table places the average retail trader at a Sharpe of about -1.0, an inexperienced discretionary retail trader at 0.1 to 0.3, and a simple one-rule mechanical system at 0.24. — [Carver](https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2020/03/how-much-risk-should-we-take.html)
- Beginners neglect or grossly underestimate transaction costs, and stop strategies in live drawdowns that looked acceptable in the backtest. — [QuantStart Part II](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-II); [QuantStart Part I](https://www.quantstart.com/articles/Successful-Backtesting-of-Algorithmic-Trading-Strategies-Part-I/)
- Davey himself reports losing 60% of an account trading moving averages and $70,000 averaging down before adopting a process. — [FinancialWisdomTV](https://www.financialwisdomtv.com/post/building-winning-algorithmic-trading-systems-kevin-davey-s-complete-framework-for-trading-success)

### Inferences
- Engine design checklist derived from Knight [T]: (1) one-command, scripted, versioned deployments with a second reviewer (for a solo owner with AI agents: an independent agent or checklist sign-off) and a post-deploy version assertion on every process; (2) delete dead code and never reuse a flag or config key for a new meaning; (3) regression-test everything still in the repository; (4) pre-trade automated limits wired into order entry (max order size, max orders per minute, max gross exposure, max daily loss) rather than dashboards a human must watch; (5) reconcile intended versus actual positions and fills continuously and halt on mismatch; (6) treat startup warnings and rejects as blocking alerts; (7) a tested kill switch that cancels open orders and stops new ones; (8) a written incident runbook whose first step is "halt", not "roll back".
- From August 2007 [P]: even a long-only, unlevered book is exposed to crowded-factor unwinds (momentum and other popular factors can gap together for days). Without leverage Kaibot cannot be forced to liquidate, which is its main protection; the design implication is to avoid tight automatic stops that would sell into a multi-day factor unwind that has historically reversed.
- From LTCM [N directly]: the leverage lesson does not apply to an unlevered account, but the liquidity and correlation lessons do: size positions against stressed, not average, liquidity and correlation.
- Kaibot's own history is a textbook instance of the retail pattern: short sample, single rule, parameters tuned in-sample, costs and execution assumptions untested until after the engine was built.

### Gaps
- No broker-published statistics on retail algorithmic trader outcomes (e.g. from Alpaca or Interactive Brokers) were found in this session; the Quantopian study is the best cohort evidence located.
- The full Khandani-Lo papers were not read (abstracts only), so their specific numbers on leverage growth and the contrarian strategy's declining returns from 1995 to 2007 are not cited.
- Forum-sourced anecdotes of live failures were not collected.

## 9. What realistic performance looks like

### Takeaway
Net of fees, the diversified systematic industry earns Sharpe ratios of roughly 0.3 to 0.6 over long periods, with multi-year flat stretches and 15 to 20% drawdowns; an excellent independent trader running a leveraged, cross-asset futures system has realised about 0.8 (before deducting a risk-free rate). A long-only stock system should be judged on drawdown and volatility relative to the index and on a small, uncertain excess return, not on absolute Sharpe ratios above 1.

### Cited Findings
- Industry benchmarks, long run (data through 31 December 2025): SG Trend Index CAGR since 2000 of 5.33% with a maximum drawdown of 20.61%; BTOP50 CAGR 4.08%, maximum drawdown 15.94%; S&P 500 Total Return CAGR 7.98%, maximum drawdown 50.95%. ### Inferences
- Calibration for Kaibot v3: (a) a backtested Sharpe above about 1.0 for a long-only stock system should be treated as a red flag for overfitting, not as success; (b) after a 50% haircut, a realistic live target is a Sharpe of roughly 0.4 to 0.7 in total-return terms, i.e. about index-like return with lower drawdown, or a low single-digit annual excess return; (c) expect at least one year in three to be flat or negative relative to the index and plan for drawdowns of 20%+ of the strategy's own equity curve.
- Because the S&P 500 compounded at about 8% a year since 2000 and returned 17.9% in 2025 alone, a long-only system that spends time in cash under a regime filter will usually lag in strong bull markets; the honest benchmark is risk-adjusted (Sharpe, maximum drawdown, time under water) against buy-and-hold SPY, set in advance.
- The CTA numbers are a ceiling reference for process quality, not for Kaibot's expected returns: those funds earn their Sharpe from cross-asset diversification, shorting and leverage, none of which Kaibot has.
- Paper trading results will overstate live results unless fills are modelled pessimistically; the gap between Carver's slippage with and without an execution algorithm (0.55% versus 1.34% a year) shows execution quality alone can be worth about 0.8% a year even at low turnover.
